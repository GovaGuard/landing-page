Business associate agreements: a guide for EU health-tech vendors

HIPAA forbids a US covered entity from handing protected health information (PHI) to a vendor without a signed business associate agreement: 45 CFR 164.502(e) makes the contract a precondition, 164.504(e) dictates what it must say, and 164.308(b) repeats the demand for electronic PHI. So when your first US healthcare customer sends a BAA, it is not boilerplate – it is the legal gate to the deal, and signing it changes your regulatory status.

Business associate agreement (BAA) is the written contract HIPAA requires before a business associate may create, receive, maintain, or transmit protected health information for a covered entity or another business associate. Its mandatory provisions are fixed by 45 CFR 164.504(e); signing one makes your HIPAA duties contractual and statutory at the same time.

By Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · Last reviewed: September 17, 2026

// LIABILITY CHAIN

Why the BAA lands on your desk – and what it drags behind it

If you touch PHI on behalf of a covered entity or another business associate – even encrypted, even transient – you are a business associate. Since the HITECH Act, that status carries direct statutory liability: HHS's Office for Civil Rights can penalize you for your own Security Rule and breach-notification failures, independent of anything the contract says. The BAA adds the contractual layer on top.

The chain does not stop with you. Every subcontractor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI for you is itself a business associate, and 45 CFR 164.502(e)(1)(ii) requires you to bind it with its own BAA before any PHI flows. Covered entity to you, you to your cloud provider, your cloud provider onward: each link needs its own agreement, and each link inherits breach-reporting duties upstream. A gap anywhere in that chain is your compliance problem, not your subcontractor's.

The wider US market context – risk analysis, safeguards, and the HITRUST question – is covered on our HIPAA & HITRUST readiness page; when procurement asks for a certification on top of the BAA, start with HITRUST tiers.

// REQUIRED PROVISIONS

What every BAA must contain – and what to check before signing

HHS publishes sample business associate agreement provisions that track the mandatory content of 45 CFR 164.504(e). Most drafts you receive are built on them. The rows below are the provisions that matter most for a vendor – read your draft against each one.

Provision What the clause requires What to check before signing Negotiation note
Permitted uses & disclosures Describes exactly how you may use or disclose PHI; anything beyond the contract or the law is prohibited Does the scope cover what your product actually does – support access, analytics, de-identification? Get every intended use in writing now; adding one later means re-papering the agreement
Safeguards Appropriate safeguards to prevent impermissible use or disclosure; Security Rule compliance for electronic PHI (164.308(b), 164.314(a)) Whether the clause imports controls beyond the Security Rule, e.g. the customer's own security addendum Anchor commitments to your documented risk analysis, not an open-ended "best practices" promise
Breach reporting to the covered entity Report impermissible uses and disclosures, security incidents, and breaches of unsecured PHI to the covered entity The deadline: the statute allows up to 60 days from discovery, but drafts often demand 24–72 hours Agree a clock your incident process can meet; carve routine unsuccessful scans out of "security incident"
Subcontractor flow-down Ensure any subcontractor handling PHI agrees to the same restrictions, via its own BAA (164.502(e)(1)(ii)) Whether your current subprocessors will sign equivalent terms, and if customer consent is needed for changes Sign downstream BAAs first – you cannot flow down what your cloud provider will not accept
Access & amendment support Make PHI available for individual access, amendment, and accounting of disclosures (164.524, 164.526, 164.528) Whether you can technically export, amend, and log the relevant records within the clause's timelines Commit to assisting the covered entity – not to answering individuals directly
Termination & return/destruction The covered entity may terminate for material breach; at the end, PHI is returned or destroyed if feasible What "return or destroy" means for backups, replicas, and immutable storage in your architecture Invoke the infeasibility option up front: extended protections instead of impossible deletion promises

Two further mandatory elements rarely need negotiation but should not surprise you: the agreement must make your books and records available to HHS on request, and where you carry out a covered entity's own Privacy Rule obligation, you must comply with the Rule as it applies to that obligation.

// EU SPECIFICS

What changes when the vendor sits in the EU

Your GDPR processor discipline helps, but it does not substitute. An Article 28 data processing agreement and a BAA are different instruments with different mandatory content – PHI is defined differently than personal data, and a signed DPA satisfies none of the 164.504(e) provisions. Expect to run both contracts over the same data flows.

The breach clocks differ, so run both. HIPAA's outer statutory limit for reporting a breach of unsecured PHI to the covered entity is 60 days from discovery – often contractually shortened. GDPR gives the controller 72 hours to the supervisory authority and requires you, as processor, to notify the controller without undue delay. One incident, two regimes, two notification runbooks firing in parallel.

Finally, expect data-location questions GDPR never prepared you for – in reverse. US customers will ask where PHI is hosted, whether EU-based staff access production data, and whether your EU cloud region and support model appear in the BAA's subcontractor list. HIPAA does not prohibit offshore processing, but many covered entities restrict it contractually; surface your hosting reality before the redline round, not after.

// PROCESS

From first BAA request to signature – without overpromising

  1. Map the PHI before you answer

    Run a data flow analysis: where PHI would enter, rest, and leave your systems, and which subcontractors sit in the path. This confirms whether you are a business associate at all – and if you are, it defines the scope everything else hangs on.

  2. Read the draft against the HHS baseline

    Compare the customer's draft against HHS's sample provisions. Everything beyond the baseline – shortened breach clocks, audit rights, indemnities, offshore restrictions – is commercial, not statutory, and therefore negotiable.

  3. Check every promise against a real control

    For each clause, name the process that fulfils it: the reporting clock your incident response actually achieves, the export path for access requests, the deletion story for backups. Where no control exists, negotiate the clause or build the control – never sign the gap.

  4. Paper the downstream chain, then sign

    Execute BAAs with every subcontractor touching PHI – cloud providers publish standard ones – and align their terms with what you are about to promise upstream. Then sign, file, and put the agreement's duties into your compliance calendar.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Do we need a BAA if we only host encrypted PHI?

Yes. If you create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI on behalf of a covered entity or another business associate – even encrypted, even without decryption keys – you are a business associate and a BAA is required. HHS treats persistent storage of PHI as business associate activity; only genuine conduit services carrying transient transmissions fall outside it.

Is signing a BAA enough to be HIPAA compliant?

No. The BAA is a precondition, not the compliance itself. You still owe a documented Security Rule risk analysis, implemented safeguards, and breach readiness – and since HITECH you are directly liable to regulators for those, regardless of contract wording. There is also no official "HIPAA certification": no HHS certificate exists, so no seal can replace the work.

Does our GDPR data processing agreement cover the BAA requirement?

No. An Article 28 DPA and a BAA regulate the same relationship under different laws with different mandatory content. Neither satisfies the other; a US covered entity cannot accept a DPA in place of the 164.504(e) provisions. If PHI of US patients flows through EU infrastructure, run both agreements side by side over the same data flows.

Can we negotiate a BAA, or is it take-it-or-leave-it?

The statutory core – permitted uses, safeguards, reporting, flow-down, return or destruction – is fixed. Everything the customer adds on top is negotiable: reporting deadlines shorter than the 60-day statutory limit, audit rights, indemnities, offshore restrictions. Large covered entities move less on wording, but they routinely accept deadline and carve-out adjustments a vendor can justify.
