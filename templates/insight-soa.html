// INSIGHTS · ISO 27001

The Statement of Applicability, done right

The Statement of Applicability (SoA) is required by ISO/IEC 27001 clause 6.1.3 d). It lists every control your organisation considers necessary, states whether each Annex A control applies, and justifies every inclusion and exclusion. It is the one ISMS document that certification auditors and enterprise customers both read line by line – and the one most often written carelessly.

Statement of Applicability is the mandatory ISO/IEC 27001 document (clause 6.1.3 d) that lists the necessary information security controls, records for each Annex A control whether it is applicable, justifies every inclusion and exclusion, and states the implementation status – linking the risk treatment plan to the control set the auditor certifies against.

By Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · Last reviewed: September 17, 2026

// FUNDAMENTALS

The bridge between risk treatment and Annex A

Clause 6.1.3 of ISO/IEC 27001 describes a sequence: you assess risks, select risk treatment options, determine the controls those treatments need, and then compare your control set against Annex A to verify nothing necessary has been overlooked. The SoA is where that comparison is recorded. It is not a copy of Annex A – it is the documented outcome of your risk treatment decisions.

That direction matters. Controls do not apply because they appear in Annex A; they apply because a risk, a legal obligation, or a contractual commitment makes them necessary. An SoA that reads as if Annex A was worked through top to bottom, with the risk register bolted on afterwards, tells the auditor exactly that story – and invites the question every certification audit circles back to: which risk does this control actually treat?

Since the 2022 revision, Annex A contains 93 controls in four themes (organisational, people, physical, technological), with implementation guidance in ISO/IEC 27002:2022. Your certificate references your SoA by version and date – which makes it, in a very literal sense, the document your certification is scoped by.

// STRUCTURE

What a defensible SoA row looks like

The standard does not prescribe a format, but four columns have become the working consensus: the control, whether it applies, why, and how far implementation has progressed. The justification is the column that earns its keep – it should point at something specific: a risk ID, a contract clause, a legal requirement. Example rows from a SaaS company:

Control Applicable? Justification Implementation status
A.8.24 Use of cryptography Yes Treats risk R-014 (interception of customer data); required by customer DPAs. TLS 1.3 in transit, AES-256 at rest per crypto policy CP-02. Implemented
A.5.19 Information security in supplier relationships Yes Treats risk R-027 (subprocessor failure); production depends on two cloud providers and a managed payroll service. Implemented
A.7.4 Physical security monitoring Yes Treats risk R-031 (unauthorised office access to workstations handling client data). Partially implemented – access logging live, camera coverage planned Q1 2027
A.8.30 Outsourced development No All software development is performed in-house by employed staff; no development is outsourced. Exclusion reviewed at the annual management review. Not applicable

Note what the excluded row does: it names a verifiable fact, not an opinion, and commits to re-checking it. An exclusion is a claim the auditor can test – the moment a contractor commits code, that row is wrong and the auditor knows your SoA is not maintained.

// PITFALLS

The four mistakes we see in almost every first draft

  1. Copy-paste justifications

    Ninety rows reading “applicable – best practice” justify nothing. A justification that fits every control fits no control. Each row should trace to a risk, a law, or a contract; if you cannot name one, you have not decided the control is necessary – you have assumed it.

  2. Exclusions without rationale

    “Not applicable” with an empty justification cell is a nonconformity waiting to be written up. Clause 6.1.3 d) explicitly requires justification for exclusions. Defensible exclusions state a fact about the organisation that makes the control moot – and that fact gets re-verified when the business changes.

  3. The SoA drifts from the risk treatment plan

    The two documents describe the same decisions from different angles, so they must agree. A control marked “implemented” in the SoA while the treatment plan shows the treating measure as open – or a treated risk whose control appears nowhere in the SoA – is the fastest finding an auditor can raise.

  4. Versioning chaos

    Your certificate cites a specific SoA version. If sales sends customers one version, the auditor holds another, and the wiki hosts a third, nobody can say which control set you actually stand behind. One owner, one version history, one approval per change – treat it like the controlled document it is.

// AUDIENCE

Who reads it besides the auditor

Enterprise security reviews increasingly ask for the SoA alongside the certificate, because the certificate alone says little: it proves an ISMS exists, while the SoA shows which controls are inside it. Expect reviewers to quote your own rows back at you – an exclusion of supplier controls will surface in the next vendor questionnaire, and a “partially implemented” row becomes a follow-up question with a deadline attached.

That is not a reason to write a vaguer SoA; it is a reason to write an honest one. A precise document with a few well-explained partial rows reads as maturity. A document claiming 93 fully implemented controls reads as fiction – to customers and auditors alike.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Is the Statement of Applicability mandatory for ISO 27001?

Yes. Clause 6.1.3 d) of ISO/IEC 27001 requires you to produce a Statement of Applicability containing the necessary controls, justification for their inclusion, their implementation status, and justification for excluding any Annex A control. Without it, there is no certification audit – the certificate itself references the SoA version it was issued against.

Can we exclude Annex A controls?

Yes – Annex A is a reference set, not a compliance checklist. Any control can be excluded if it is genuinely not needed to treat your risks or meet legal and contractual obligations. The exclusion must be justified with a verifiable fact, such as no outsourced development taking place, and reviewed whenever the business changes.

How often should the SoA be updated?

Whenever risk treatment decisions change – after a risk assessment, a scope change, a new supplier, or an incident – and at minimum as part of the annual management review cycle. Each update should produce a new approved version, since surveillance audits check that the SoA still matches the risk treatment plan and reality.

Do we have to share the SoA with customers?

Not under the standard, and many organisations treat it as confidential. In practice, enterprise customers frequently request it during security reviews, and refusing outright slows deals. A common middle ground is sharing it under NDA, or providing a summary that lists applicable control themes and exclusions without internal risk IDs.
