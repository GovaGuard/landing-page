ISO 42001 vs the EU AI Act: evidence, not conformity

As of September 17, 2026, no harmonised standard under the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) has been cited in the Official Journal — which means no certificate, ISO/IEC 42001 included, grants a presumption of conformity with the Act. Yet buyers keep asking “Are you AI Act compliant?” and vendors keep answering with a 42001 certification plan. The two are related. They are not the same thing. This page draws the line precisely.

ISO/IEC 42001 is the international, certifiable standard for an AI management system (AIMS). It requires an organization to govern how it develops and uses AI — risk and impact assessments, data governance, human oversight, lifecycle controls, continual improvement — verified by an accredited third-party audit on a three-year certification cycle with annual surveillance.

By Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · Last reviewed: September 17, 2026

// THE CONFUSION

One question, two different answers

The AI Act is a binding EU regulation, directly applicable in every Member State, enforced by market surveillance authorities with fines of up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of global turnover. ISO/IEC 42001 is a voluntary management-system standard, audited by certification bodies whose harshest sanction is withdrawing the certificate. A regulation defines what you must do; a standard describes how a disciplined organization does it.

The confusion is understandable because the timing overlaps. The Act phased in from August 2024: prohibitions and AI-literacy duties since February 2, 2025, general-purpose AI model rules since August 2, 2025, and the Article 50 transparency duties since August 2, 2026. The bulk of the high-risk obligations was deferred by the Digital Omnibus on AI, agreed in May 2026: Annex III systems to December 2, 2027, AI embedded in Annex I regulated products to August 2, 2028.

Our position, consistent with our ISO 42001 consulting practice: certification is strong evidence of the risk-management discipline the Act expects — not automatic legal conformity. Anyone who tells you a certificate settles the legal question is selling you a shortcut that does not exist.

// THE OVERLAP

Where an AIMS operationalizes Act duties

The substantive overlap is real. The Act’s high-risk requirements — risk management (Article 9), data and data governance (Article 10), record-keeping and logging (Article 12), transparency to deployers (Article 13), human oversight (Article 14) — map onto exactly the disciplines a 42001 AIMS institutionalizes: documented risk and impact assessments, data quality controls, logging, oversight roles, and supplier management. An organization running a working AIMS has the operating machinery most Act duties assume.

The European standards are converging in the same direction — but 42001 is not (yet) one of them. CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 is drafting the harmonised standards under standardisation request M/613; the Commission concluded that ISO/IEC 42001:2023 as written is not aligned with the quality-management system Article 17 requires, so JTC 21 developed a dedicated European standard, prEN 18286, instead of adopting 42001 directly.

Status as of this review: EN 18286 (quality management, Article 17) is at formal vote; the risk-management (prEN 18228), logging (prEN 18229-1), and cybersecurity (prEN 18282) drafts are at the enquiry stage; others remain in drafting. CEN-CENELEC targets availability of the prioritized deliverables in Q4 2026. Until a standard is cited in the Official Journal, nothing — European or ISO — grants the Article 40 presumption of conformity.

// THE GAP

What no certificate covers

Three Act obligations sit entirely outside any management-system audit. First, classification: the Act assigns duties by role — provider, deployer, importer, distributor — and by risk class of each individual AI system. A 42001 auditor checks your process for classifying; the legal classification itself is your call and your liability.

Second, the conformity route for high-risk systems: conformity assessment, technical documentation per Annex IV, the EU declaration of conformity, and CE marking — for Annex I products typically through a notified body. This is product-compliance work, closer to CRA readiness than to management-system certification, and no AIMS certificate substitutes for it.

Third, registration: providers of high-risk Annex III systems must register them in the EU database before placing them on the market, and certain deployers have registration and fundamental-rights impact assessment duties of their own. These are administrative acts with deadlines — a certificate neither performs nor replaces them.

// SEQUENCE

Classify first, certify second

  1. Classify under the Act

    Inventory every AI system, determine your role per system, and classify against the prohibited, high-risk, and transparency categories. Document the reasoning — a defensible “not high-risk” conclusion is a compliance artifact in itself, and the December 2027 deadline rewards early clarity.

  2. Build the AIMS to operationalize

    Stand up the 42001 management system around your actual obligations: risk and impact assessments, data governance, logging, human oversight, supplier controls. Built this way, the AIMS becomes the operating machinery for Act duties instead of a parallel paperwork exercise.

  3. Certify when it earns its keep

    Pursue the accredited certificate when a buyer, tender, or board asks for independently verified evidence. That is what the certificate is: strong, auditable proof of discipline — valuable precisely because it is honest about not being legal conformity.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does ISO 42001 certification make us EU AI Act compliant?

No. No certificate grants automatic conformity with the Act. What ISO 42001 does is operationalize the risk-management, documentation, and oversight discipline the Act expects — and the harmonised standards under development are converging in the same direction. Treat the certificate as strong evidence, not a legal shield.

Is ISO 42001 a harmonised standard under the AI Act?

No, and as of September 2026 no standard is: nothing has been cited in the Official Journal, so nothing grants the Article 40 presumption of conformity. The Commission found ISO/IEC 42001:2023 misaligned with the Act’s Article 17 quality-management requirements, so CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 is finalizing a dedicated European standard, prEN 18286, currently at formal vote.

When do the AI Act high-risk obligations actually apply?

Later than originally planned. Prohibitions and AI-literacy duties have applied since February 2, 2025, GPAI model rules since August 2, 2025, and Article 50 transparency duties since August 2, 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI, agreed in May 2026, deferred high-risk obligations for stand-alone Annex III systems to December 2, 2027 and for Annex I embedded systems to August 2, 2028.

Should we classify under the Act or start ISO 42001 first?

Classify first. Your role and risk classification determine which Act duties bind you — and therefore what the management system must operationalize. Building the AIMS around a documented classification avoids a paperwork exercise that misses your real obligations. Certify afterwards, when a buyer or tender values the independent evidence.
