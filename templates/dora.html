// FRAMEWORK

DORA compliance for financial entities and their ICT providers

The Digital Operational Resilience Act has been in force since 17 January 2025. The surprise is not the banks — they saw it coming and staffed for it. The surprise is their vendors: every SaaS company, cloud provider, and software house with a financial-sector customer inherits DORA duties through contract clauses, usually as a dense addendum with a signature deadline.

We bring both sides — the regulated entity and the ICT provider — up to the operational-resilience bar, with a control set that survives contact with an audit.

Reviewed by Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · September 2026

// SCOPE

Who is in scope

01

Financial entities

Banks, insurers, investment firms, payment institutions, crypto-asset providers — more than 20 entity types are directly regulated. They carry the full obligation set: risk framework, incident reporting, resilience testing, and third-party oversight.

  • Direct supervision by financial regulators
  • All five DORA pillars apply
  • Proportionality by size and risk profile
02

ICT third-party providers

Any SaaS, cloud, or software vendor serving a financial entity. You are not directly regulated — your obligations arrive via Article 30 contract addenda, and your customer's regulator reads your name in their register of information.

  • Duties imposed by contract, not statute
  • Audit and access rights for customers
  • Exit and sub-outsourcing commitments
03

Critical ICT providers

Providers designated as critical by the European Supervisory Authorities fall under direct EU oversight, including inspections and penalties. The designation is rare — but the contractual ripple it creates hits everyone upstream in the supply chain.

  • ESA designation, direct oversight
  • Hyperscalers and core-banking platforms first
  • Flow-down requirements to subcontractors
// STRUCTURE

The five pillars

  1. ICT risk management framework

    A documented framework covering identification, protection, detection, response, and recovery — with governance attached. The board is personally accountable: management approves the framework, reviews it, and is expected to understand it.

  2. ICT incident reporting

    Incidents must be classified against harmonized criteria, and major ones reported to the regulator on fixed timelines — initial notification, intermediate report, final report. That requires a classification scheme and a runbook, not a best-effort email.

  3. Digital operational resilience testing

    A testing program proportionate to size and risk: from baseline vulnerability assessments and scenario tests up to threat-led penetration testing (TLPT) for significant entities, run against live production systems.

  4. ICT third-party risk

    A register of information covering every ICT contract, mandatory contractual provisions per Article 30, and management of concentration risk. This pillar is the one that turns vendors into de-facto DORA subjects.

  5. Information sharing

    Voluntary exchange of cyber threat intelligence between financial entities, within arrangements that protect confidentiality and competition. The only pillar that is optional — and the one most firms defer.

// ARTICLE 30

The vendor reality: Article 30 addenda

Here is what lands on a SaaS vendor's desk: audit and access rights for the customer and its regulator, a documented exit strategy, transparency over sub-outsourcing, duties to support the customer during incidents, and a catalogue of security requirements — forty pages, non-negotiable in tone, signature requested by quarter end.

Refusing means losing the customer. Signing blind means promising audit rights you have no process for and exit support you cannot operate. Neither is a strategy.

We translate the addendum into a control set you can actually run — and where an ISMS already exists, we map DORA obligations onto it instead of building a parallel program. Most of what Article 30 demands is a delta on top of our ISO 27001 practice, not a second compliance universe.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does DORA apply to us if we're not a bank?

If financial entities are among your customers: practically yes. DORA does not regulate you directly, but Article 30 obliges your customers to push its requirements downstream through contract — audit rights, incident support, exit strategies, security requirements. The obligations are contractual rather than statutory, but the commercial consequence of ignoring them is the same.

We're ISO 27001 certified — are we DORA-ready?

A certified ISMS is a strong foundation — roughly 70 percent of the way there on risk management. The deltas are specific: regulator-facing incident reporting with fixed timelines and classification criteria, a formal operational resilience testing regime, and the register of information for ICT third-party contracts. Those are additions to your ISMS, not a reason to rebuild it.

What is TLPT and do we need it?

Threat-led penetration testing per the TIBER-EU framework: intelligence-driven red-team exercises against live production systems, at least every three years. Only significant financial entities are directly obliged. Vendors get pulled in indirectly — as critical providers of a tested entity, your systems can be inside the exercise scope, and you will be asked to cooperate.

What happens if we ignore a DORA addendum?

Your financial customer cannot legally keep you as a provider without the mandatory contractual provisions in place. Expect remediation deadlines first, contract termination second. And because every ICT contract sits in the customer's register of information, your gap is visible not just to their procurement team but to their regulator.
