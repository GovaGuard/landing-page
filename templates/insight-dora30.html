DORA Article 30: the clauses your financial customers must make you sign

DORA – Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 – has been in force since 17 January 2025, and Article 30 prescribes, clause by clause, what every contract between a financial entity and an ICT service provider must contain. That is why a 40-page addendum landed on your desk: your customer is not negotiating taste, they are discharging a statutory duty. Refusing the catalogue means, for them, not being allowed to use you.

DORA Article 30 is the clause catalogue of Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 that financial entities must write into every contract with an ICT service provider. Article 30(2) sets baseline provisions for all ICT services; Article 30(3) adds stricter ones – full SLAs, audit rights, exit strategies – where the service supports a critical or important function.

By Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · Last reviewed: September 17, 2026

// WHY NOW

Why a 40-page addendum arrived

DORA regulates banks, insurers, payment institutions and investment firms directly – not their vendors. But Article 30 obliges those customers to push a fixed set of contractual provisions downstream to every ICT provider they use. You inherit the duties by contract: audit and access rights, exit strategies, sub-outsourcing transparency, incident support.

The commercial mechanics are unforgiving. A financial entity that cannot get the clauses signed must walk away, because its regulator will read the contract register – the register of information – and ask where the missing provisions are. So the addendum is not an opening position. It is the entry ticket to the account, and the only real question is whether you can operate what it says. That framing anchors our DORA practice.

// THE CATALOGUE

The clause catalogue, tier by tier

Article 30 splits in two. Article 30(2) applies to contracts for all ICT services; Article 30(3) adds provisions where the service supports a critical or important function of the customer. Which tier you are in is the customer's assessment, not yours – but it decides most of the addendum's weight.

Tier 1 – Article 30(2): baseline for all ICT services

What the clause demands What it means operationally Before you sign
Service description & locations – a clear, complete description of all functions and services, the regions or countries where they are provided, and advance notice of location changes An accurate service catalogue, a maintained list of processing locations including subcontractors, and a change-notification process Can you actually enumerate every subprocessor location today – and detect when one changes?
Data protection provisions – availability, authenticity, integrity and confidentiality of data, including personal data Documented, evidenced controls: encryption, backups, access management – your ISMS control set mapped to this wording Check the clause against your Statement of Applicability, not against your marketing page
Access, recovery and return – data back in an easily accessible format on insolvency, resolution or contract termination A tested export and hand-back path that works without goodwill or a running commercial relationship Run the export once before promising it in writing
Service level descriptions – including updates and revisions Versioned, published SLAs whose metrics your monitoring actually measures If your dashboards do not measure what the SLA states, one of the two has to change
Incident assistance – support when an ICT incident relates to your service, at no additional cost or at a cost agreed ex ante A duty to staff your customer's incident, on their timeline, alongside your own response Decide the cost model now – the regulation itself allows a pre-agreed fee instead of free
Cooperation with authorities – full cooperation with the customer's competent and resolution authorities A financial regulator can reach you through your customer; someone must own that conversation Name the contact and rehearse the process before the first request arrives
Termination rights – termination rights and minimum notice periods in line with supervisory expectations Offboarding on notice periods you did not choose, including for regulatory reasons on the customer's side Align the notice periods with your real offboarding capacity, then negotiate the numbers

Article 30(2) also expects providers to take part in the customer's ICT security awareness programmes and digital operational resilience training – a small clause that still needs an owner on your side.

Tier 2 – Article 30(3): additions for critical or important functions

What the clause demands What it means operationally Before you sign
Full SLAs with performance targets – precise quantitative and qualitative targets enabling monitoring and corrective action Continuous measurement the customer can see, with defined consequences when targets are missed Only commit to targets your telemetry already produces
Notice periods & reporting duties – notify any development with a material impact on your ability to deliver Proactive disclosure triggers: incidents, key-staff loss, subcontractor changes, financial distress Define internally what counts as material, or every hiccup becomes a notification debate
Contingency plans & security measures – implement and test business contingency plans; maintain appropriate ICT security measures Documented, exercised continuity and disaster-recovery evidence, not a plan in a drawer Your last DR test report is the proof this clause will be audited against
TLPT participation – participate and fully cooperate in the customer's threat-led penetration testing Your production systems can fall in scope of a regulator-framed red-team exercise Agree scoping, safeguards and scheduling mechanics before the first test, not during it
Unrestricted audit & inspection rights – complete access, inspection and audit rights for the customer, its appointees and its regulator, including on site Hosting customer and authority audits as a recurring operational process with evidence ready The right is unrestricted; the modalities – frequency, pooled audits, reliance on certifications – are yours to negotiate
Exit strategies & transition periods – an adequate transition period with continued service while the customer migrates to another provider or in-house A documented exit plan you keep serving under, possibly after the relationship has soured Cost, duration and staffing of transition assistance belong in the contract, in numbers
// NEGOTIATION

What is negotiable – and what is not

The catalogue itself is not negotiable. Your customer cannot lawfully drop the audit-rights clause or the exit strategy, because Article 30 obliges them to include it – asking them to delete it is asking them to breach DORA. Redlines against the existence of a clause waste a negotiation round and signal that you have not read the regulation.

The modalities are genuinely negotiable: notice-period lengths, audit mechanics such as pooled audits or reliance on ISO 27001 and SOC 2 reports for routine assurance, cost allocation for incident assistance and transition support – the regulation explicitly permits costs agreed ex ante. Negotiate there, in numbers, and concede the catalogue gracefully.

One warning: do not sign clauses your organization cannot operate. A promised audit process with no owner, an exit plan nobody has tested – that is the contractual cousin of the paper-ISMS warning signs, and it surfaces at the worst possible moment: during your customer's supervisory review.

// PLAYBOOK

From addendum receipt to signature

  1. Map clauses to actual capabilities

    Walk the addendum clause by clause against what your organization demonstrably does today: export paths, audit hosting, DR test evidence, notification triggers. Not what the policy says – what the last twelve months of evidence show.

  2. Build the gap list

    Every clause lands in one of three buckets: operable today, operable with defined work, or not operable as drafted. The third bucket is your negotiation agenda; the second is your project plan. Writing this down takes days, not months.

  3. Turn gaps into a remediation plan

    For each gap: owner, effort, date. An existing ISO 27001 ISMS carries most of the load – the deltas cluster around exit strategies, audit logistics and customer-facing incident support, and they extend your ISMS rather than replace it.

  4. Negotiate the modalities

    Accept the catalogue, negotiate its mechanics: notice periods you can staff, pooled or certificate-based routine audits, pre-agreed cost schedules for incident and transition assistance, TLPT scoping safeguards. Bring your gap list – it makes your asks concrete and credible.

  5. Sign what you can operate

    Sign when every clause has an owner and an evidence trail, and the remediation plan for the rest is agreed with the customer. A signature backed by operations survives the customer's audit; a signature backed by hope becomes their finding – and your churn risk.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does DORA apply to my SaaS company directly?

Usually not. DORA regulates financial entities, and it has been in force since 17 January 2025. Your obligations arrive by contract: Article 30 forces your financial customers to impose the clause catalogue on you. The duties are contractual rather than statutory – but the commercial consequence of refusing them is losing the customer.

What is the difference between Article 30(2) and 30(3)?

Article 30(2) is the baseline for all ICT service contracts: service description and locations, data protection, data return, service levels, incident assistance, cooperation with authorities, termination rights. Article 30(3) adds provisions where the service supports a critical or important function: full SLAs with performance targets, reporting duties, tested contingency plans, TLPT participation, unrestricted audit rights and exit strategies.

Can I negotiate Article 30 clauses out of the addendum?

The catalogue itself, no – your customer is legally required to include it, so deleting a clause asks them to breach DORA. The modalities, yes: notice-period lengths, audit mechanics such as pooled audits or reliance on certifications for routine assurance, and cost allocation – the regulation itself allows incident and transition assistance at costs agreed ex ante.

Does my ISO 27001 certificate cover the Article 30 requirements?

It carries most of the load – the data-protection, security and continuity clauses map well onto an operated ISMS, and certificates can serve as routine audit evidence. The deltas are specific: documented exit strategies, customer-facing incident support, audit-hosting logistics and TLPT cooperation. Those extend your ISMS; they are not a reason to rebuild it.
