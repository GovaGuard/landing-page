Open source and the CRA: when involvement becomes commercial activity

The Cyber Resilience Act does not ban open source. Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 applies only to products with digital elements made available on the EU market in the course of a commercial activity. Contributing code to a project not under your responsibility carries no CRA duties – Recital 18 says so in plain words. The lines that matter run elsewhere: between contributing, stewarding, and monetizing.

Open-source software steward is defined in Article 3(14) CRA: a legal person, other than a manufacturer, whose purpose is to systematically provide sustained support for the development of specific free and open-source products intended for commercial activities, and which ensures the viability of those products. Foundations are the archetype the regulation had in mind.

By Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · Last reviewed: September 17, 2026

// THE FEAR

Why maintainers thought the CRA was coming for them

Early CRA drafts triggered genuine alarm across the open-source world: if every piece of software on the EU market needs CE marking, does a hobby maintainer in Hamburg become a regulated manufacturer? Foundations and advocacy groups – the Eclipse Foundation, the Linux Foundation, the Open Source Initiative among them – pushed back, and the final text answered with a layered carve-out rather than a blanket exemption.

The result is precise but easy to misread in both directions. Recital 18 establishes that free and open-source software falls in scope only when supplied in the course of a commercial activity – and that development circumstances, financing, manufacturer contributions, and regular releases do not by themselves make an activity commercial. Recital 15 lists what does: charging a price, paid support beyond cost recovery, platform monetization, or mandatory personal-data processing.

So the fear was wrong, but complacency would be too. The same regulation that shields the contributor makes the monetizer a full manufacturer – and puts every commercial vendor on the hook for the open-source components inside its own product.

// DECISION PATH

Three roles, three very different rulebooks

Your role CRA position What applies
Contributor or maintainer of a project not monetized by you Out of scope (Recital 18) No CRA obligations. Contributing code to products not under your responsibility is explicitly excluded; sponsorship received and regular releases do not change that.
Foundation or entity systematically supporting OSS intended for commercial use Open-source software steward (Art. 3(14)) Light-touch regime under Article 24: documented cybersecurity policy, cooperation with market surveillance authorities, limited reporting duties. Exempt from administrative fines (Art. 64(10)).
Monetizer or integrator: paid distributions, paid support, OSS shipped inside a commercial product Manufacturer Full CRA duties for the product placed on the market: Annex I essential requirements, SBOM, vulnerability handling, ENISA reporting, technical documentation, CE marking.

Two boundary notes the regulation states directly. Not-for-profit development stays non-commercial as long as all earnings after costs serve not-for-profit objectives. And an OSS component supplied for integration by other manufacturers counts as "making available on the market" only if the original manufacturer monetizes it (Recital 18). Dual licensing is not named in the text; treating the paid-license branch as commercial activity is interpretation – but it follows squarely from Recital 15 and is the consensus reading.

// STEWARD REGIME

What Article 24 actually asks of stewards

  1. A verifiable cybersecurity policy

    Stewards must put in place and document a policy that fosters secure development and effective vulnerability handling by the project's developers – including documenting, addressing and remediating vulnerabilities, encouraging voluntary reporting under Article 15, and sharing vulnerability information within the community.

  2. Cooperation with market surveillance authorities

    On request, stewards cooperate to mitigate risks posed by the software they support, and provide that policy documentation to the authority in an easily understood language, on reasoned request.

  3. Scoped reporting duties

    The Article 14(1) duty to report actively exploited vulnerabilities applies only to the extent the steward is involved in development. Severe-incident reporting under Article 14(3) and (8) applies only where incidents hit infrastructure the steward provides for development – build systems, forges, repositories.

No CE marking, no Annex I conformity, no fines: Article 64(10)(b) excludes stewards from administrative fines entirely. How far the steward category reaches beyond foundations – Recital 19 mentions entities developing OSS "in a business context" – remains one of the genuinely interpretive edges; expect Commission guidance to sharpen it. What is clear: the steward regime covers foundations and similar supporters, not vendors shipping products.

// VENDOR ANGLE

Your product's OSS dependencies are your CRA problem

Most companies reading this are not maintainers or foundations – they are vendors whose commercial product is eighty percent open-source dependencies. The CRA is unambiguous here: the manufacturer duties attach to the product you place on the market, dependencies included. Article 13(5) demands due diligence when integrating third-party components, open source explicitly among them.

Concretely, that means an SBOM covering your dependency tree, a vulnerability handling process that watches those dependencies for the whole support period, and the ability to report actively exploited vulnerabilities in your product within 24 hours – even when the vulnerable code was written by a volunteer. We cover the pipeline mechanics in SBOM in CI and the full obligation set in our CRA readiness practice.

Article 25 gives this a future assist: the Commission may establish voluntary security attestation programmes for free and open-source software, so integrators can lean on attested components for their due diligence. Those programmes do not exist yet – until they do, dependency diligence is manual and yours.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does the CRA ban or regulate open-source development?

No. The CRA regulates products made available on the EU market in the course of a commercial activity. Recital 18 excludes contributors to projects not under their responsibility, and non-monetized open-source supply is not a commercial activity. Development circumstances, financing, and regular releases do not change that.

Is paid support around our open-source project commercial activity?

Usually yes. Recital 15 names charging for technical support beyond mere cost recovery as an indicator of commercial activity – alongside charging a price, monetizing a platform, or requiring personal-data processing. If the answer is yes, you are a manufacturer for the product you supply, with full CRA duties.

What must an open-source software steward do?

Under Article 24: maintain a documented, verifiable cybersecurity policy covering secure development and vulnerability handling, cooperate with market surveillance authorities on request, and meet scoped reporting duties tied to their involvement in development. Stewards face no CE marking and are exempt from administrative fines under Article 64(10).

Do donations or corporate sponsorship make a project commercial?

Not by themselves. Recital 18 states that financial support from manufacturers, or manufacturers contributing code, does not make an activity commercial. Recital 15 draws the line at accepting donations exceeding costs with the intention of making a profit – donations without profit intent are explicitly not commercial activity.
