SBOM in CI: meeting the CRA's software bill of materials duty

The Cyber Resilience Act – Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 – makes the SBOM a legal requirement, not a nice-to-have. Annex I, Part II obliges manufacturers to identify and document vulnerabilities and components in their products, including by drawing up an SBOM in a commonly used, machine-readable format covering at the very least the top-level dependencies. This article shows how to generate that SBOM where it is actually true: in your CI pipeline, on every build.

SBOM (software bill of materials) is a machine-readable inventory of the components, libraries and dependencies inside a software artifact, including versions and origin metadata. Under the Cyber Resilience Act, manufacturers must draw one up covering at minimum the top-level dependencies of each product with digital elements, as part of the Annex I vulnerability handling requirements.

By Nils Carstensen & Manuel Eckarth · Last reviewed: September 17, 2026

// WHY NOW

Why the SBOM stopped being optional

Two forces converged. First, the regulator: the CRA's Annex I, Part II vulnerability handling requirements apply to every product with digital elements placed on the EU market, and the SBOM is their documented foundation. Market surveillance authorities may request it. The main obligations bite from 11 December 2027, but the reporting duties under Article 14 already apply since 11 September 2026 – the clock is running today.

Second, your customers: enterprise procurement and security teams now ask for SBOMs in vendor questionnaires as routinely as they ask for certificates. A manufacturer who cannot produce one on request loses deals before any authority ever asks. Where your product sits in the CRA at all – default, important or critical class – is covered on our CRA readiness page.

// FORMATS

CycloneDX or SPDX: picking a format

The CRA does not mandate a specific format – it requires “a commonly used and machine-readable format”. In practice that means one of two: CycloneDX or SPDX. Both satisfy the duty. Pick based on your toolchain, not on the regulation.

  CycloneDX SPDX
Origin OWASP; standardized as Ecma International ECMA-424 Linux Foundation; standardized as ISO/IEC 5962
Design center Security use cases: vulnerability correlation, VEX, attestations License compliance and provenance, extended toward security
Ecosystem Broad scanner and dependency-track support; JSON and XML Strong in open-source license tooling and large-vendor programs; multiple serializations

Honest answer: if your primary consumer is a vulnerability management workflow, CycloneDX is usually the shorter path; if license compliance tooling already speaks SPDX in your organization, stay there. Most generators emit both, and converters exist. Choosing is a Tuesday decision – generating consistently is the actual work.

// PIPELINE

Wiring the SBOM into CI, step by step

An SBOM produced once a year in a spreadsheet is stale before it is saved. The only SBOM that matches what you ship is the one your pipeline generates from the build artifact itself. Tools like syft, cyclonedx-cli and the native SBOM support in modern build systems (Maven, Gradle, npm, Go, cargo ecosystems all have generators) make this a pipeline step, not a project.

  1. Generate per build artifact

    Run the generator against the built artifact – container image, binary, package – not just the manifest files. Scanning the artifact catches what actually ships, including base-image layers. One SBOM per artifact, produced in the same job that builds it, versioned with the same identifier.

  2. Attach it to the release

    Publish the SBOM alongside the artifact: as a release asset, an OCI attestation, or a registry attachment. The binding matters – an SBOM you cannot trace to a specific shipped version answers no question an authority or customer will ever ask.

  3. Diff against known vulnerabilities

    Feed the SBOM to a scanner such as grype or query osv.dev – both consume CycloneDX and SPDX directly. Run this on every build and on a schedule against released SBOMs, because new CVEs land against components you shipped months ago.

  4. Define a gate-or-alert policy

    Decide in writing what blocks a build (known-exploited, critical with a fix available) and what raises a ticket. A pipeline that fails on every CVE gets bypassed within a week; a pipeline that never fails is decoration. The policy is the engineering artifact auditors ask about.

  5. Retain and deliver

    Keep SBOMs for every supported release for as long as the product is on the market, and establish the delivery channel now: a customer portal, a signed URL per release, or on-request with a defined SLA. Deciding this during an incident is too late.

// OPERATIONS

An SBOM nobody monitors is paper

The CRA does not ask for an SBOM as a document – it asks for vulnerability handling, and the SBOM is that process's input. Annex I, Part II also requires manufacturers to address and remediate vulnerabilities without delay and to provide security updates. Since 11 September 2026, Article 14 adds the hard deadline: an early warning for an actively exploited vulnerability within 24 hours to the CSIRT and ENISA via the single reporting platform.

That 24-hour clock is the operational test of your pipeline. When a scanner flags a component in a released SBOM, someone must be able to answer within hours: which products, which versions, which customers, is it exploitable in our usage. Common failure modes we see:

  1. SBOM only at release time

    Quarterly releases mean quarterly visibility. Generate per build and rescan released SBOMs continuously – the CVE feed does not wait for your release train.

  2. Missing transitive dependencies

    The CRA minimum is top-level dependencies, but exploited components are frequently transitive. Where your tooling can resolve the full graph – and for most ecosystems it can – include it.

  3. No link from SBOM hit to the 24-hour clock

    A scanner alert that lands in a muted channel never reaches the person who owes ENISA an early warning. Wire scanner findings into the same incident process that owns Article 14 reporting, with named ownership and a tested escalation path.

The SBOM is one item on the manufacturer's list – the conformity assessment, technical documentation and CE marking sit around it. We walk through that sequence in the CE-marking checklist.

// FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Does the CRA require a specific SBOM format?

No. Annex I, Part II of Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 requires a “commonly used and machine-readable format” covering at least the top-level dependencies. Both CycloneDX (OWASP, ECMA-424) and SPDX (Linux Foundation, ISO/IEC 5962) satisfy this. Choose based on your existing toolchain; the Commission may specify further details in implementing acts.

Do I have to publish my SBOM?

The CRA requires you to draw up and maintain the SBOM as part of the technical documentation; it does not oblige general publication. Market surveillance authorities can request it, and customers increasingly demand it contractually. A defined delivery channel – portal, per-release asset, or on-request – covers both without open publication.

Is generating an SBOM enough for CRA compliance?

No. The SBOM is one requirement inside Annex I, Part II, which also demands handling and remediating vulnerabilities without delay, providing security updates, and a coordinated disclosure policy. Since 11 September 2026, actively exploited vulnerabilities must be reported within 24 hours under Article 14. The SBOM is the input that makes those duties operable.

Are top-level dependencies really sufficient?

Legally, the regulation's stated minimum is top-level dependencies. Operationally, most exploited components are transitive, so an SBOM limited to the top level will miss the hits that trigger your reporting duties. Where your build tooling can resolve the full dependency graph, include it – the cost is near zero in CI.
